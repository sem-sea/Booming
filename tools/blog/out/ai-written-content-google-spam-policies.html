Key takeaways

Google's spam policies name using generative AI to create many pages without adding value as scaled content abuse. AI-assisted content is allowed; volume without original value is the risk. Google's June 2026 spam update rolled out worldwide in two days (Search Engine Roundtable).

Many B2B marketing teams now use AI to draft content, and many publish more than ever. Google's line is clear in its own documentation: what counts is whether pages help people, not how they were made. Its guidance on generative AI content asks site owners to check accuracy and add value (Google Search Central).

Google's human quality raters follow the same logic. Since the January 2025 update to their guidelines, pages made with "little to no effort, little to no originality, and little to no added value" get the Lowest rating, and using automated tools as a low-effort way to produce many pages is named as an example (Search Engine Roundtable).

Enforcement comes in waves. The June 2026 spam update started on 24 June and finished on 26 June, applied to all languages, and Google said recovery can take many months (Search Engine Roundtable).

Does Google penalize AI-generated content?

Google's position is that AI use is not the problem: content made at scale to rank, without added value, breaks its spam policies.

An AI-drafted article that an expert has checked, extended with real examples and kept current is judged on the same quality signals as any other article. A hundred AI-generated pages that rephrase what already ranks are what the policies target.

What is scaled content abuse?

Scaled content abuse is Google's term for generating many pages mainly to manipulate rankings, whether by AI, scraping or people, without helping users.

How do raters judge AI-assisted pages?

Rater judgment means human reviewers score pages for effort, originality and added value; copied or auto-generated pages with little of each get the Lowest rating.

Rater scores do not change rankings directly. Google uses them to test whether its ranking systems work. But they show what Google wants those systems to reward. The guidelines also say generative AI can support high-quality work; the tool itself does not set the rating.

How should a B2B team use AI for content safely?

Using AI safely means a human owns each page: AI drafts, while an expert adds experience, checks every fact and decides what gets published.

  1. Start from a buyer question, not a keyword list.
  2. Write the brief with a first-hand angle: a project, a number, an opinion only your team has.
  3. Let AI draft and research structure, definitions and sources.
  4. Have an expert edit and add examples, data and judgment.
  5. Check every number against its original source and link it.
  6. Publish under a named author with relevant experience.
  7. Set a pace you can review, and update or remove posts that no longer help anyone.

If you publish in the EU, the same editorial control also covers you under the AI Act. Our post on the EU AI Act for marketing teams explains when AI-written text needs a label.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need to label AI-written blog posts?

Not for Google's spam policies. Under the EU AI Act, a label is needed only for AI text on matters of public interest that no human reviewed under editorial responsibility.

How many posts a week is safe?

Google gives no number. Publish what your experts can review properly. Quality and topic fit count, not volume.

Can a site recover after a spam update?

Yes, but Google says it can take many months. Improve or remove low-value pages and keep publishing helpful content.

Should we delete old AI-written posts?

Review them. Improve posts that serve your buyers, merge thin ones, and remove or noindex those that add nothing.

Does Google's guidance apply to AI search answers too?

AI Overviews and AI Mode draw on Google's index, so pages that fall in rankings also lose visibility there.
